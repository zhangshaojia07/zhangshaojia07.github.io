Yesterday, as team **tokenmaxxers**, Artem Kordonets, Reet Kothari and I finished **12th out of 364 teams (top 4%)** at UKIEPC 2026, and 7th among Cambridge teams, with 10 problems solved. I also took first blood on Problem M, solving it just 16 minutes into the contest, before any of the other 300+ teams.

{{< figure src="team-photo.jpeg" alt="team-photo" position="center" caption="Team photo (from left to right: Reet, yours truly, Artem)" captionPosition="center" >}}

For context, I hadn't touched CP for a year and a half, apart from one single contest (THUPC 2026). I signed up for UKIEPC for one reason only: to rekindle my passion for the game.

I grabbed two teammates before I even knew there was a team-matching contest that helped contestants find partners, so, technically speaking, I missed the chance to team up with more experienced competitors. Both are Lucy CS freshers who had never touched competitive programming, so I ended up doubling as coach and contestant: I brought them up to speed along the way and wrote almost all the code myself. They were great company and even chipped in with ideas on a couple of problems, but the heavy lifting was largely a one-person job.

During the contest, where AI and Google were strictly off the table (a little ironic for a team called tokenmaxxers), I coded almost non-stop for five hours and was completely exhausted and starving by the end.

The winning team solved 12 problems, two more than us. A few problems stood out. I had the right idea for Problem L and thought it through for 30 minutes, starting to code just before the scoreboard froze, but after five submissions and plenty of debugging I still hadn't found the final bug: I'd forgotten to check for extra disjoint cycles once I'd detected exactly one rooted tree. Problem B I misjudged badly: I couldn't make sense of the sample, assumed it was hard, and only realised afterwards that it was actually simpler than L. I solved both of them today. Problem D I deliberately left on the table even though I already knew its solution, because it's hard and time-consuming; knowing some classic problems without being able to solve them on the spot is somehow beneficial, since it lets me spot the problems that stop a full solve in a contest. Still, the core algorithm behind D is amazing.

I'm slightly disappointed that the organisers didn't hand out a first-blood award for each problem.

A huge thanks to Jane Street and Capital One for sponsoring the event.
